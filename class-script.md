# Class script — quadrat sampling

**You drive `widget.html` on the projector. This document is what you say; the widget is
what they look at.** Students then do the practice themselves in `quadrat-practice.html`
(see the last section).

Blockquoted lines are the argument, not a teleprompter — say them your way. The **Ask**
lines are the ones worth keeping verbatim, because the wording is doing work.

Running time: about 25 minutes for Parts A–C, plus 10 for Part D if you want it.

**Setup:** open `widget.html`, click through to **section 2, Walkthrough**, and leave it on
step 1. You never need to show section 1 on screen — its text is Part A below, and the
walkthrough's first square is a better thing to be looking at while you say it.

---

## Part A — The square

*On screen: walkthrough, step 1. One quadrat, no grid yet.*

> You cannot count every plant in the schoolyard. Nobody can. So ecologists do something
> that feels like cheating and isn't: they measure a small area *exactly*, and then scale
> it up.

> That small area is a **quadrat** — this square, one metre on each side. Whatever is
> inside the frame gets recorded. Whatever is outside does not exist for that reading. The
> whole method depends on you being ruthless about that line.

> What you record is **percent cover**: looking straight down, what fraction of the ground
> is hidden by each kind of plant? Not how many plants — how much *ground they cover*.

**Ask:** *Why don't we just count the plants?*

Let them get there. The answer you want is that grass has no countable individuals — you
cannot point at where one plant stops and the next starts. That's not a technicality, it's
the reason the whole method is built on area instead of counts.

**Hold back the 1% grid trick.** It is the reveal in Part B and it lands much harder if
they have already struggled without it.

### Where does the square go?

> This is where a survey gets wrecked before it starts. If you put the quadrat where the
> interesting plants are, your data describes your taste, not the schoolyard.

**Ask:** *You're surveying the strip from the mowed field to the hedge. Where does quadrat
number one go?*

Offer these four and make them commit before you rule:

1. Somewhere with a good mix of species, so the data isn't boring
2. At the 0 m mark on the tape, whatever happens to be there
3. Wherever the ground is flat enough to lay the frame down properly
4. Each group member picks a spot and we average them

**Ruling — number 2.** Decide the position *before* you look at the ground. Stretch the
tape, sample at a fixed interval, take whatever the square lands on — including the boring
squares and the empty ones.

> The empty quadrats are data. "We chose good spots" is the fastest way to make a survey
> worthless, and it is exactly what a marker looks for.

Number 4 is worth a moment if someone picks it, because averaging *sounds* rigorous. Four
biased choices averaged together are still biased — averaging removes noise, not bias.

## Part B — Reading one square

*Work through the walkthrough one step at a time. The widget asks for four numbers; in class
you take them from the room instead of from one student's keyboard.*

### Step 1 — Look before you write

> Straight down at the square. No angles, or you count the same plant twice.

Name what's there together: lawn turf, some round-leaved weed, a couple of scuffed patches
of dirt.

**Ask:** *No grid, no counting. What percent of this square is bare dirt? Call out a number.*

**Write four or five of their guesses on the board and leave them there.** This is the whole
setup for step 4 — don't skip it, and don't reveal anything yet. Expect a wide spread;
that spread is the point.

### Step 2 — Lay the grid over it

*Turn the grid on.*

> Ten by ten. A hundred cells. So **one cell is exactly one percent** — count the cells a
> species fills and you have read the percentage off the ground instead of guessing it.

> In the field this is string tied across the frame, or you just picture it.

Let the change in the room happen. The square goes from "hard to judge" to "countable" and
they can feel it.

### Step 3 — One species at a time

*Advance — everything but dollarweed fades out.*

> Dollarweed only. Ignore everything else. That fading is what your attention is supposed
> to be doing on its own.

> Two half-cells make one whole cell.

**Ask:** *How many cells?*

**Actual: 10%.** Anyone within two or three is doing well.

### Step 4 — Now bare ground

*Advance.*

**Ask for a count first, then reveal. Actual: 10%.**

Now go back to the board.

> Look at what you guessed before we had the grid.

This is the moment the lesson turns. The gut estimates are usually spread from about 5% to
30% for something that is actually 10%; the counted numbers cluster within a few points.
Nobody has to be told the grid is worth the trouble — they can see it in their own numbers.

> Dirt counts as cover, by the way. Bare patches are data — they are what mowing,
> trampling and shade look like as a number.

### Step 5 — Don't count the turf

*Advance.*

> St. Augustine is most of the square. Counting sixty-odd cells is slow and you will lose
> your place halfway. Every bit of ground is covered by something — so the dominant species
> gets whatever is left over.

**Ask:** *We said 10 and 10. So what is the grass?*

**80%.** One subtraction instead of sixty cells, and it adds no error — the error was
already in the two numbers they counted.

### Step 6 — Check it totals 100

> 80 plus 10 plus 10. If your column doesn't total 100, you have double-counted an overlap
> or missed some ground.

> You fix that standing on the spot. Never back in the classroom — by then you cannot
> check anything, and you will be tempted to just make the numbers work.

That's one quadrat: three numbers, adding to 100, in about a minute — and the slowest part
was the species nobody counted.

## Part C — One square isn't enough

*Switch to section 4. The whole lawn from above.*

> Somewhere in this lawn, dollarweed covers some true percentage of the ground. It's a
> number you would never know without counting every square metre of it.

*Drop one quadrat.*

**Ask:** *Would you hand that in as the answer for the whole lawn?*

*Drop a few more, one at a time, so they watch the number lurch around.* Individual
quadrats in this lawn run from **0% to 97%** — same lawn, same method, honest measurements
every time.

*Then drop ten at once, and again.*

The running mean settles near **18%**, the true value. Twenty quadrats usually lands within
a point or so.

> No single quadrat was reliable. The average of enough of them is. That is the entire
> argument for doing this properly instead of walking out, having a look, and writing down
> what you reckon.

**Ask:** *So how many is enough?*

There's no clean answer, which is worth saying out loud — you take more quadrats until the
running mean stops moving. That is a real judgement working ecologists make, not a number
from a textbook.

## Part D — Walk a transect *(optional, ~10 min)*

*Switch to section 5. The site widens out: a road down one edge, a pond off the other.*

> Random quadrats told you *how much*. They cannot tell you *why*, because the moment you
> average them you have thrown the position away.

*Record the eight quadrats along the tape, one at a time, and let the stacked chart build.*

Bare ground runs **52% beside the road and 0% at the pond end**. Nutsedge does the exact
opposite, **0% to 92%**. St. Augustine peaks around **7 m**, out where it is neither driven
over nor waterlogged.

*Now switch the toggle to "Quadrats at random" and record eight more.* Same site, same
eight squares, same effort — and the chart is unreadable noise.

**Ask:** *What would this chart look like if we'd run the tape parallel to the road instead
of across it?*

That question is the check on whether they actually understood it. The answer — flat, boring,
no zonation, because you never crossed the gradient — is also the reason we'll pick the
line carefully when we go outside.

## Then: hand it to them

Students open the practice widget on their own devices:

`https://USERNAME.github.io/REPO/edrm/quadrat-practice.html`

Four quadrats along a schoolyard tape. Everyone gets the **same four**, so the work is
gradeable and they can't compare answers usefully. They type a name at the top, estimate
percent cover for every row, and each column must total 100% before it will let them check.

They hand in the summary line at the bottom: name, quadrats recorded, mean error in
percentage points, and a quality band.

- **≤6 points — Field-ready.** Genuinely good; two trained surveyors differ by more.
- **≤12 — Usable.**
- **>12 — Redo.** They eyeballed whole squares instead of counting cells.

Their work is saved in the browser, so a closed tab doesn't cost them the period. "Start
over" clears it if they want another run at it.

**Still to do out loud, separately:** the four judgment calls — the plant half in the frame,
the branch rooted outside, the plant nobody can name, the column that totals 128%. Those are
written up with the rulings and the wrong answers students actually offer in `plan.md`.
Settle them as a class and put the agreed rules on every data sheet before going outside.
